When you buy a bottle of Coca-Cola, you are paying for a drink, but the company behind it is also selling something less visible: **brands, concentrates, marketing and a worldwide distribution system**.

That distinction matters. The Coca-Cola Company produces concentrates and syrups for many independent bottling partners. Those partners make, package and distribute much of the finished product. Coca-Cola also operates some finished-product businesses itself. It is not simply a factory that directly bottles everything it sells.

> **The big idea:** Separating a brand owner from a bottling operator can reduce the amount of physical equipment required to earn each dollar of profit. But a familiar brand is not the same as guaranteed cash flow.

## How the money moves

The simplified chain is:

**Consumers → shops and restaurants → bottlers → concentrate and syrup purchases from Coca-Cola.**

Cash does not necessarily move along that chain in one neat transaction, and Coca-Cola also has direct finished-product sales. But the important economic point is that **brand ownership, manufacturing and distribution can be performed by different companies**.

A concentrate business can generate attractive margins while leaving many bottling plants, delivery vehicles and local distribution costs with its bottling partners.

## The income statement: revenue barely rose, profit jumped

All figures are **US$ billions**, rounded, for years ended **31 December**.

| Income statement | FY2024 | FY2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net operating revenue | 47.06 | 47.94 |
| Gross profit | 28.74 | 29.54 |
| Operating income | 9.99 | 13.76 |
| Net income attributable to shareholders | 10.63 | 13.11 |

Source: [Coca-Cola FY2025 Form 10-K, consolidated income statements](https://investors.coca-colacompany.com/filings-reports/all-sec-filings/content/0001628280-26-010047/ko-20251231.htm).

Revenue grew **1.9%**, while operating income rose about **37.7%**. That sounds like spectacular efficiency—but there is an accounting trap here.

Operating margin rose from roughly **21.2% to 28.7%**. Part of the increase came from **much lower other operating charges** than in 2024. In 2024, Coca-Cola recorded a large remeasurement charge related to the previously agreed payment for its **fairlife** acquisition. That charge did not repeat at the same scale in 2025.

So the correct reading is **not** “Coca-Cola sold 2% more drinks and suddenly became 38% better at operating.” Some improvement reflected pricing and concentrate volumes; much of the reported comparison also reflects unusual charges. Understanding the difference between recurring performance and accounting effects is central to good business analysis.

Gross margin was **61.6%** in FY2025 (29.544 ÷ 47.941), meaning about 62 cents of gross profit remained per dollar of revenue *before* overhead, marketing and other operating costs. That high percentage is consistent with a valuable brand-and-concentrate model, but it is a group-wide measure, not a pure concentrate-margin disclosure.

## The balance sheet: where does the brand appear?

| Balance sheet, US$ billions | Dec 2024 | Dec 2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash and cash equivalents | 10.83 | 10.27 |
| Property, plant and equipment | 10.30 | 9.61 |
| Trademarks with indefinite lives | 13.30 | 12.53 |
| Goodwill | 18.14 | 15.49 |
| Total assets | 100.55 | 104.82 |

Physical property and equipment represented only about **9.2% of total assets** at the end of 2025 (9.613 ÷ 104.816). Compare that with BHP's much larger proportion in the mining case study.

The accounting balance sheet also includes **$12.53 billion of indefinite-lived trademarks** and **$15.49 billion of goodwill**. Goodwill largely arises from business combinations, and trademark carrying values follow accounting rules. Neither line tells you what the entire Coca-Cola brand would sell for today. Famous brands built internally often have economic value not captured by a simple accounting asset number.

This is the difference between **book value** and **economic value**.

## The cash flow statement: profitable does not mean unlimited cash

| Cash movement, US$ billions | FY2024 | FY2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Operating cash flow | 6.81 | 7.41 |
| Purchases of property and equipment | 2.06 | 2.11 |
| **Operating cash flow less CapEx** | **4.74** | **5.30** |
| Dividends paid | 8.36 | 8.78 |

FY2025 showed approximately **$5.30 billion** of operating cash after routine property and equipment purchases. Yet dividends paid were about **$8.78 billion**—more than this simple free-cash-flow measure.

How is that possible? Cash available for dividends can come from existing balances, asset sales, investments and financing, not just the current year's operating cash flow. It does mean an investor should not mechanically assume that dividends were fully covered by this one cash-flow calculation.

The particularly important wrinkle is the **$6.17 billion fairlife contingent-consideration payment**, settled in March 2025. The associated cash movement affected operating cash flow, even though it was acquisition-related and not an ordinary annual cost of producing beverages. Removing such an unusual payment can help show recurring cash-generation capacity—but adjustments should be disclosed, never quietly hidden.

## Connect the three statements

Suppose Coca-Cola transfers a bottling operation to an independent partner. In a simplified case, the company might report *less finished-drink revenue* but a *higher percentage operating margin*, because it now sells concentrates instead of doing all manufacturing and delivery. Property and equipment could decline, while future spending on plants and trucks may be reduced.

That is **capital efficiency**, not necessarily shrinking demand. It also depends on the price of the transaction, bottler performance and contract economics; refranchising does not automatically create value.

## What to watch next

Look at **concentrate volumes, pricing versus inflation, margins adjusted for unusual charges, cash conversion, dividends versus available cash and the health of bottling partners**. A seemingly stable consumer brand can still face currency, input-cost, health-trend and distribution risks.

**In one sentence:** Coca-Cola demonstrates how brands can generate high margins, while its FY2025 results demonstrate why profit growth and cash flow must be read alongside unusual accounting and acquisition payments.
