Yesterday we considered how Amazon can generate substantial operating profits while spending even more on servers and data centres. **NVIDIA sits on the other side of many such investments.** It sells much of the technology that makes AI computing possible.

That relationship gives us a remarkable business contrast: an infrastructure owner spends billions on long-lived assets and hopes to recover the cost over years. A technology supplier can earn a large profit *when the infrastructure is built*. The supplier still takes risks, but its asset base and cash-flow profile can look completely different.

> **The central idea:** Where a company sits in the value chain can matter as much as the size of the market it serves. NVIDIA captures the value of powerful processors, networking and software without having to own every chip factory or AI data centre.

All financial amounts in this article are **US dollars**. The latest quarterly comparison is **Q3 FY2027 (ended 25 October 2026) versus Q3 FY2026 (ended 26 October 2025)**. NVIDIA's fiscal year ends in January, so the label FY2027 refers to a quarter in calendar 2026. The latest quarterly figures were published on **17 November 2026**.

## 1. How NVIDIA makes money

A customer using an AI chatbot might pay a subscription to an application provider. That provider pays for computing capacity, either from its own servers or a cloud operator. Cloud providers and other infrastructure builders buy processors, networking equipment and integrated systems. NVIDIA earns money by supplying computing technology into this chain.

The business is not limited to a graphics chip. NVIDIA combines processor architecture, networking, systems, the **CUDA** software environment and developer tools. Once a customer has invested heavily in optimising workloads for that ecosystem, switching hardware may require software rewrites, testing and engineering time. That creates a competitive advantage, although competing hardware and open software alternatives continue to develop.

NVIDIA also uses a largely **fabless manufacturing model**. It designs chips and platforms but relies on manufacturing and packaging partners for much of the physical production. It does not avoid supply-chain risk; it chooses not to own every expensive fabrication facility.

During Q3 FY2027, total revenue reached **$115.844 billion**, up **103%** from $57.006 billion in the comparable quarter. The **Data Center** business alone brought in **$107.6 billion**—approximately **92.9% of company revenue**. The remaining roughly $8.24 billion came from other activities, including gaming, professional visualisation and automotive.

Put simply, NVIDIA has become highly dependent on demand for advanced computing infrastructure. That concentration helps explain its growth *and* its exposure to changes in AI investment spending.

[Source: NVIDIA's Q3 FY2027 earnings announcement](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-Financial-Results-for-Third-Quarter-Fiscal-2027/).

## 2. Income statement: extraordinary margins from scarce technology

An income statement shows revenue earned and expenses recognised over a period. It does **not** show exactly when every customer pays or supplier receives cash.

| GAAP income statement, US$ billions | Q3 FY2026 | Q3 FY2027 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 57.01 | **115.84** |
| Cost of revenue | 15.16 | 29.17 |
| **Gross profit** | **41.85** | **86.68** |
| Research and development | 4.71 | 7.45 |
| Sales, general and administrative | 1.13 | 1.70 |
| **Operating income** | **36.01** | **77.52** |
| **Net income** | **31.91** | **65.29** |

*Source: [NVIDIA's SEC-filed Q3 FY2027 supplemental financial statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm), condensed consolidated income statements. Figures rounded from US$ millions.*

The outstanding number is the **74.8% gross margin**. NVIDIA spent about $29.17 billion on the direct costs of generating $115.84 billion in quarterly sales. The difference—roughly $86.68 billion—was gross profit.

Another way to read this is to follow **$100 of revenue**:

| Stage | Amount remaining from $100 |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue earned | $100.00 |
| After direct production costs: gross profit | **$74.82** |
| After R&D, sales and administration: operating profit | **$66.92** |
| After other income and income taxes: net profit | **$56.36** |

That final $56.36 is a **net profit margin of 56.4%**. It is an accounting margin, not an assertion that $56 in cash instantly arrives in NVIDIA's bank account.

Why can margins be so high? A chip's selling price is determined by more than the manufacturing bill. Customers are also buying computational performance, power efficiency, software compatibility and the value of running demanding workloads at scale.

### Operating leverage

Revenue rose **103.2%**, but R&D spending rose approximately **58.4%**, from $4.705 billion to $7.454 billion. Total operating expenses rose about **56.8%**, while operating income increased **115.3%**.

As sales grew faster than operating expenses, operating margin climbed from **63.2%** to **66.9%**. This is *operating leverage*: the ability to spread engineering, software and organisational expenses across substantially more revenue.

It works in reverse, too. If demand weakens, R&D and some operating costs cannot be reduced immediately. Profit could then fall faster than sales.

## 3. Balance sheet: not all company wealth is a factory

A balance sheet is a photograph taken on a specific date. It records assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity. To understand NVIDIA's expansion, compare **25 January 2026** with **25 October 2026**, nine months later.

| Selected balance-sheet items, US$ billions | Jan 2026 | Oct 2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash and cash equivalents | 10.61 | **29.09** |
| Marketable debt securities | 39.07 | 24.42 |
| Marketable equity securities | 12.89 | 44.22 |
| Accounts receivable | 38.47 | **81.03** |
| Inventories | 21.40 | **39.72** |
| Property and equipment, net | 10.38 | **19.23** |
| Non-marketable securities | 22.25 | 65.53 |
| **Total assets** | **206.80** | **372.19** |
| Accounts payable | 9.81 | 21.07 |
| Total current liabilities | 32.16 | 54.36 |
| Long-term debt | 7.47 | 32.37 |
| **Shareholders' equity** | **157.29** | **266.67** |

*Source: [SEC-filed Q3 FY2027 supplemental information](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm), consolidated balance sheets. Selected components; totals include additional line items not displayed here.*

**Physical property and equipment represented just 5.2% of total assets** in October 2026: $19.227 billion divided by $372.188 billion. That is striking for a business selling physical computing products. It is consistent with an asset-light *manufacturing ownership* strategy, but it does not mean the business has no capital commitments. NVIDIA still needs inventory, engineering facilities, manufacturing capacity from partners and substantial investment in its ecosystem.

There are two less obvious balance-sheet stories.

**First, customer payments are arriving later than some revenue is recognised.** Accounts receivable reached **$81.03 billion**, up from $38.47 billion in January. Receivables are amounts customers owe on recognised sales. They are assets but **not yet cash**. NVIDIA's growth makes collections and customer-credit exposure increasingly important.

**Second, liquidity is more than the bank balance.** October current assets were **$221.994 billion**, compared with **$54.361 billion** of current liabilities. That produces **$167.633 billion in working capital**, with a current ratio of **4.08×**. It suggests substantial near-term financial resources. But inventory must be sold, receivables collected and securities converted before every dollar becomes spendable cash.

NVIDIA's share price and market capitalisation are **not** part of that cash calculation. They describe the price investors assign to the equity, not an operating account that management can withdraw from.

## 4. Cash flow: converting profits into money

The cash flow statement brings the story back to reality. It shows how much cash came in from operations and where cash went to invest or finance the company.

| Q3 cash flows, US$ billions | Q3 FY2026 | Q3 FY2027 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| GAAP net income | 31.91 | **65.29** |
| GAAP operating cash flow | 23.75 | **48.25** |
| Purchases of property, equipment and intangibles | (1.64) | (4.42) |
| Related principal payments | (0.02) | (0.05) |
| **NVIDIA-defined free cash flow** | **22.09** | **43.78** |

*Sources: [SEC-filed cash flow statement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm) and [NVIDIA's earnings-release reconciliation](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-Financial-Results-for-Third-Quarter-Fiscal-2027/). Free cash flow is a **non-GAAP**, company-defined measure.*

NVIDIA defines its free cash flow as:

**Operating cash flow − asset-related purchases − related principal payments**

In Q3 FY2027, that was **$48.253B − $4.422B − $0.054B = $43.777B**.

This means NVIDIA reported roughly **90.7 cents of free cash flow for every dollar of operating cash flow** in that quarter. This impressive conversion partly reflects the fact that NVIDIA does not itself bear the full cost of building the data centres that run its hardware. However, the number is a period-specific calculation, not an everlasting guarantee.

There is another useful contrast: **net income of $65.29 billion exceeded operating cash flow of $48.25 billion**. Operating cash flow was approximately **73.9% of net income**.

What explains the gap? Working-capital movements are part of the answer. In the quarter, changes in receivables used approximately **$18.05 billion** of cash and inventories used **$8.15 billion**, while accounts payable released **$4.42 billion** of cash. Gains on equity investments and other non-cash accounting adjustments also affect the reconciliation. Across the first **nine months** of FY2027, net equity-security gains were **$25.47 billion**; that nine-month figure must not be mistaken for a single-quarter gain.

**A sale can raise profit before payment arrives. A gain on an investment can raise accounting income without being an operating cash receipt.** The cash-flow statement separates these effects.

### Capital allocation: what happens to the surplus?

NVIDIA reported returning about **$28.1 billion to shareholders** through repurchases and dividends during Q3 FY2027. That is roughly **64.2%** of its $43.78 billion of reported quarterly free cash flow. It is a useful scale comparison, **not** a formal reconciliation of the company's final bank balance.

Repurchases can boost earnings per share without improving underlying profit: $100 million of profit across 100 million shares equals $1 per share, whereas the same profit across 90 million shares equals about $1.11. But buying shares creates value only when the price and alternative uses of cash justify the decision.

## 5. One transaction, three financial statements

Consider a simplified **$100 million processor sale**, with equipment costing **$25 million**. The manufacturer is paid before delivery; the customer pays 60 days after delivery. Ignore taxes, overhead, financing and other transactions.

| Event | Income statement | Balance sheet | Cash flow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NVIDIA pays $25M for inventory | No revenue or profit yet | Cash falls $25M; inventory rises $25M | Operating cash outflow of $25M |
| NVIDIA delivers and invoices $100M | Revenue +$100M; cost of sales $25M; gross profit **$75M** | Inventory falls $25M; receivables rise $100M | No additional cash collected yet |
| Customer pays 60 days later | No new revenue from this payment | Cash rises $100M; receivable falls $100M | Operating cash inflow of $100M |

Across all three stages, the simplified transaction generates **$75 million in gross profit** and **$75 million in net operating cash inflow**. But those results are recognised at different times.

That is the key to understanding a booming supplier business: **reported revenue, money owed by customers and money actually collected are related—but never interchangeable.**

## 6. Why the same AI boom gives Amazon and NVIDIA different economics

Amazon and other infrastructure owners purchase and operate data centres, servers, networking and related assets. They need future customers to use that capacity profitably over years. NVIDIA sells technology that enables that infrastructure and often records the revenue earlier in the investment cycle.

Both companies benefit from demand for AI computing, but **NVIDIA's manufacturing model requires substantially less directly owned physical equipment than a major cloud provider's infrastructure model**. That helps explain why the earlier Amazon study showed negative free cash flow from extraordinary investment spending while NVIDIA reported substantial positive free cash flow.

The relationship is not guaranteed to stay favourable forever. If cloud operators find that demand or returns are lower than expected, they can slow orders. Then the supplier's excellent margins and growth rates may come under pressure.

## 7. Risks hidden behind extraordinary profitability

A profitable business can still be vulnerable. NVIDIA's most important exposure is **customer demand for new AI infrastructure**: Data Center represented nearly 93% of sales this quarter. Any pause in spending by large cloud companies or AI infrastructure builders could have an outsized impact.

There are also **alternative accelerators** and custom chips from major customers, **manufacturing and advanced-packaging dependencies**, **export restrictions**, power and data-centre capacity limits, and the challenge of keeping new architectures competitive. Strong sales may also increase working-capital requirements and supply commitments.

NVIDIA's November 2026 outlook called for **$130 billion in Q4 FY2027 revenue, plus or minus 3%**, with GAAP gross margins expected between **71% and 72%**. That is a **management forecast**, not a realised result, and the quarter includes an additional week. The outlook assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China.

As longer-term context, completed FY2026 revenue was **$215.94 billion**, compared with **$130.50 billion** in FY2025. FY2026 net income was **$120.07 billion**, versus **$72.88 billion** the prior year. These are **full-year** figures and should not be added to or directly compared with a single quarter without adjusting the periods.

[Source: NVIDIA FY2026 annual report, Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000021/nvda-20260125.htm).

## The distinction investors cannot afford to miss

If investors trade NVIDIA shares for a higher price tomorrow, the resulting increase in **market capitalisation** does not deposit that amount into NVIDIA's operating bank account. Ordinary secondary-market share trades transfer money between investors. New share issuance, by contrast, can raise capital for a company.

NVIDIA's **$29.09 billion in cash and equivalents** at 25 October 2026 is a reported balance-sheet item. A hypothetical trillion-dollar increase in the market value of its shares is **not** an increase in that cash balance.

Market value asks what investors are willing to pay for ownership of expected future profits. The financial statements ask how the actual company earns, owns, owes and moves money. They answer different questions.

> **One-sentence takeaway:** NVIDIA illustrates how a business controlling scarce, high-value computing technology can capture extraordinary margins without owning most of the infrastructure customers must build to use it.
