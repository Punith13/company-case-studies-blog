A mining company is unlike Costco or Coca-Cola. BHP must find ore, build mines, operate enormous equipment, transport the output and sell it into global commodity markets. **Much of the cost is committed before the selling price is known.**

That makes mining a useful introduction to *operating leverage*: profits can move much more than revenue when selling prices change.

> **The big idea:** Good production and low costs matter, but even a well-run miner can have a difficult year if commodity prices turn against it.

## Where BHP's money comes from

BHP supplies iron ore, copper, steelmaking coal and other resources to industrial customers. An iron-ore buyer typically needs material for steelmaking; a copper buyer needs metal for electrical and industrial uses.

BHP does not set the world price of copper or iron ore. Global supply, demand, inventories and trade conditions strongly affect prices. In FY2026, BHP produced roughly **two million tonnes of copper**, with copper contributing **more than half of underlying EBITDA** for the first time.

For an introductory understanding of mining profits, imagine selling **one million tonnes** of ore at **$100 per tonne**, against a simplified fixed annual operating-cost base of **$60 million**. Revenue is $100 million, profit $40 million. If prices fall to $80 without changing volumes or costs, revenue falls **20%**, but profit falls from $40 million to $20 million—**50%**. This is an illustration, not BHP's actual cost structure. In real mines some costs vary and prices may be hedged.

## The income statement: FY2026 was a stronger year

All figures are **US$ billions**, not Australian dollars. BHP is ASX-listed but reports its consolidated results in US dollars.

| Income statement | FY2025 | FY2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 51.26 | 58.76 |
| Profit from operations | 19.46 | 23.87 |
| Profit after tax, whole group | 11.14 | 13.03 |
| Profit attributable to BHP shareholders | 9.02 | 9.83 |

Source: [BHP FY2026 Annual Report, consolidated income statement](https://www.bhp.com/annualreport).

Revenue rose approximately **14.6%** and operating profit rose **22.6%**. Operating profit margin increased from about **38.0% to 40.6%**. That is a practical example of operating leverage in a favourable commodity-price environment.

But “profit attributable to shareholders” is different from group profit after tax, because some mines and subsidiaries have **non-controlling owners** entitled to a share of earnings. The report also distinguishes statutory profit from **underlying EBITDA** (FY2026: **$32.95 billion**), which adjusts for depreciation, financing, tax and selected items. Underlying EBITDA is useful for operating comparisons but is **not money in the bank**.

## The balance sheet: most assets cannot be spent tomorrow

| Balance sheet, US$ billions | Jun 2025 | Jun 2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash and cash equivalents | 11.89 | 18.53 |
| Property, plant and equipment | 76.46 | 80.05 |
| Total assets | 108.79 | 121.39 |
| Net debt (BHP's definition) | 12.92 | 8.69 |

At June 2026, about **66% of total assets** were property, plant and equipment (80.046 ÷ 121.387). Those assets include mines, processing plants and other long-lived infrastructure. They can produce future revenue but are not liquid cash.

BHP also records **provisions** for obligations such as mine closure, rehabilitation and other liabilities. They matter even if the cash is not due immediately. The financial statements show total current and non-current provisions of approximately **$20.1 billion** in FY2026; that is **not** all mine rehabilitation, so the specific notes are important.

Net debt fell by around **$4.23 billion** in FY2026, from $12.92 billion to $8.69 billion, even as BHP invested in growth. Net debt combines borrowings with cash and certain related financial instruments under BHP's definition; it is not simply the gross amount borrowed.

## The cash flow statement: capital spending consumes real money

| Cash movement, US$ billions | FY2025 | FY2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net operating cash flow | 18.69 | 21.78 |
| Purchases of property and equipment | 9.40 | 9.85 |
| Net investing cash flow | −13.35 | −12.01 |
| **BHP-reported free cash flow** | **5.34** | **9.77** |

Here is a nuance worth understanding without any homework: BHP defines its reported free cash flow as **operating cash flow plus net investing cash flow**, not simply operating cash flow minus purchases of equipment.

For FY2026, **$21.778B + (−$12.011B) = $9.767B**. The investing line also includes exploration and funding of investments, proceeds from asset sales and other transactions. That is why its published $9.77 billion differs from the more elementary $21.78B − $9.85B = **$11.93B** capital-spending-only calculation. Both tell a story, but they answer *different* questions.

BHP also paid **$6.76 billion of dividends** to its shareholders during FY2026, plus **$2.36 billion** to non-controlling interests. Dividend sustainability depends on the whole cash cycle, not just one strong commodity year.

## Connect the three statements

Suppose BHP commits $5 billion to developing a new mine. Cash can leave immediately through investing activities, while the spending is capitalised as an asset on the balance sheet if it qualifies. The mine may earn no meaningful revenue for years. Once commissioned, depreciation generally begins and reduces future accounting profit even though the large cash investment happened earlier.

A year of weak free cash flow might therefore reflect **expansion**, not business failure. Conversely, very high free cash flow could come from strong commodity prices or underinvestment, and might not persist.

## What to watch next

The useful indicators are **realised copper and iron-ore prices, production volumes, unit costs, spending required to sustain existing mines, growth projects, net debt and rehabilitation obligations**. A good mining operator can still be a volatile stock, while high commodity prices can temporarily flatter a weaker operator.

**In one sentence:** BHP illustrates why the quality of a mining business must be judged across the commodity cycle, with particular attention to cash generation after major capital investments.
